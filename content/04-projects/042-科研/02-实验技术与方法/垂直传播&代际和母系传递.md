**Vertical Transmission of Pathogens in Mammals and Birds: Mechanisms, Key Pathogens, and Host Interactions**

**Introduction**

Vertical transmission refers to the passage of pathogens from parent to offspring, encompassing several distinct routes: transplacental (across the placenta), trans-ovarial or trans-ovarian (through the ovary to the egg), and transmission via the reproductive tract, including through semen or during parturition. [^1] In mammals, the placental maternal–fetal interface provides physical, molecular, and immunological barriers that normally protect the developing fetus, yet numerous pathogens have evolved mechanisms to breach these defenses. [^1] In birds, vertical transmission typically occurs through infection of the egg — either by colonization of the ovary and oviduct before shell formation, or by penetration of the eggshell after laying. In arthropod vectors such as fleas and mosquitoes, transovarial transmission from adult to egg enables pathogen persistence across generations without requiring a vertebrate host.

This review examines vertical transmission across three categories of pathogens — arthropod-borne bacteria transmitted via fleas, viruses, and bacteria (with emphasis on Salmonella) — focusing on mechanisms of pathogen–host interaction in mammalian and avian systems.

**Vertical Transmission Mechanisms in Fleas**

Fleas are well-established vectors of *Yersinia pestis*, the causative agent of plague, and the possibility of transovarial transmission in fleas has long been debated. A landmark 2024 study by Pauling et al. published in *Nature Communications* demonstrated that laboratory-reared *Xenopsylla cheopis* (the Oriental rat flea) infected with *Y. pestis* can transmit viable bacteria from adults to eggs, and that the bacteria persist through all subsequent developmental stages — larvae, pupae, and newly emerged adults. [^2] The authors propose that transovarial transmission in fleas may contribute to *Y. pestis* persistence in the environment during periods without detectable plague activity in mammalian hosts. [^2] This finding addresses a longstanding question in plague ecology: how the pathogen survives extended inter-epizootic periods when rodent hosts show no signs of active infection.

The broader biology of flea-borne transmission was reviewed by Hinnebusch (2005), who described how *Y. pestis* adapted to flea-borne transmission through acquisition of two species-specific plasmids by horizontal gene transfer and recruitment of endogenous chromosomal genes for new vector-related functions. [^3] Transmission by flea bite depends on the formation of a bacterial biofilm in the flea proventriculus (foregut valve), which physically blocks normal blood feeding and forces regurgitation of bacteria into the mammalian host during attempted feeding. [^4][^5] The molecular and genetic basis of this biofilm-dependent transmission has been extensively characterized, including roles for the *hmsHFRS* locus, the Yersinia murine toxin, and the Hfq regulatory RNA-binding protein. [^6][^5]

Importantly, the 2024 transovarial transmission finding by Pauling et al. suggests an additional maintenance mechanism that operates independently of the classical blocked-flea model — one that could help explain pathogen persistence during quiescent periods and provide a reservoir function that has been difficult to account for through mammalian host dynamics alone. [^2]

**Vertical Transmission of Viruses**

*Avian Leukosis Virus (ALV)*

Avian leukosis virus subgroup J (ALV-J) is a highly oncogenic retrovirus that has devastated the global poultry industry since the late 1990 s, with vertical transmission serving as the primary route of congenital infection from generation to generation. [^7] Liao et al. (2022) identified a novel mechanism whereby ALV-J exploits host semen extracellular vesicles (EVs) as vehicles for vertical transmission. Semen EVs isolated from infected roosters were shown to contain ALV-J genomic RNA and viral proteins, and artificial insemination experiments confirmed that these EVs could transmit ALV-J infection to SPF (specific-pathogen-free) hens, which subsequently transmitted the virus vertically to progeny chicks. [^7] Li et al. (2017) further confirmed that hens horizontally infected with ALV-J through artificial insemination can transmit the virus to progeny through eggs. [^8]

Comparative studies have shown that vertical transmission of ALV-J appears to be more deleterious than horizontal transmission. Lin et al. (2013) found that progeny derived from vertically infected parents showed higher rates of virus shedding and viral integration in lymphocytes compared to horizontally infected birds, with lower embryo hatchability and higher progeny mortality. [^9]

Recent intervention strategies have targeted the vertical transmission route. Liang et al. (2025) demonstrated that transgenic chimeric chickens expressing recombinant nanobodies could reduce and, in high-expressing hens, eliminate vertical transmission of ALV-J. [^10] Similarly, Liao et al. (2021) showed that anti-CD 81 antibodies could inhibit ALV-J infection and prevent vertical transmission in an animal model, suggesting that the tetraspanin CD 81 plays a role in ALV-J entry and transmission. [^11]

*Classical Swine Fever Virus (CSFV)*

Classical swine fever virus provides one of the best-studied examples of transplacental viral transmission in mammals. Dewulf et al. (2001) conducted experimental infections in pregnant gilts and demonstrated that in all cases where pregnant sows were infected between days 43 and 67 of gestation, vertical virus transmission to fetuses occurred, resulting in abortion and/or mummification. [^12]

The virulence of the infecting strain strongly influences the efficiency and outcomes of transplacental transmission. Bohórquez et al. (2020) infected sows at mid-gestation with high-, moderate-, or low-virulence CSFV strains and found that the high- and moderate-virulence strains achieved efficient transplacental transmission, while the low-virulence strain generated lower viral RNA loads and a lower proportion of trans-placental transmission. [^13] Critically, transplacental transmission occurred before the onset of the maternal antibody response (which started at 14 days post-infection), suggesting that rapid viral replication outpaces the immune response and establishes fetal infection during a window of immunological vulnerability. [^13] The foetal immune system does recognize CSFV after transplacental infection, as evidenced by increases in CD 8+ T-cell subsets and IFN-alpha responses in viremic fetuses. [^13]

Vaccination strategies have been developed specifically to prevent CSFV vertical transmission. Coronado et al. (2024) demonstrated that the recombinant FlagT 4 G vaccine, given as a single dose to pregnant sows, elicited strong neutralizing antibody responses and completely prevented transplacental transmission of a highly virulent CSFV challenge strain. [^14] Similarly, plant-produced E 2 glycoprotein subunit vaccines have shown efficacy in preventing vertical transmission after double vaccination. [^15]

The epidemiological significance of transplacental CSFV transmission has been debated. Kaden et al. (2005) investigated pregnant wild boar from an endemically infected population and found no evidence that persistently infected young wild boar from transplacental transmission played a crucial role in CSFV perpetuation, suggesting other factors are more important for maintaining the virus in wild populations. [^16]

**Vertical Transmission of Bacteria: Focus on Salmonella**

Salmonella enterica remains the leading cause of foodborne zoonotic disease worldwide, and vertical transmission in poultry represents a critical route by which contaminated eggs and meat products enter the human food chain. [^17] Both horizontal (environmental) and vertical (from parent stock) transmission are primary routes of infection in chickens. [^17]

The molecular basis of Salmonella pathogenesis in poultry centers on Salmonella pathogenicity islands 1 and 2 (SPI-1 and SPI-2), which encode Type III Secretion Systems (T 3 SS) that translocate effector proteins interfering with host cell processes and facilitating bacterial internalization into Salmonella-containing vacuoles. [^17][^18] Virulence genes including *lpfA*, *csgA*, *sitC*, *sipB*, *sopB*, *sopE*, and *sivH* have been detected in Salmonella strains isolated from broiler chickens, while human clinical isolates carry an even broader array of virulence determinants. [^19]

Vertical transmission of Salmonella in poultry has been studied in both integrated production systems and experimental models. Liljebjelke et al. (2005) used pulsed-field gel electrophoresis to trace *S.* Typhimurium and *S.* Enteritidis strains through an integrated broiler production system, demonstrating that identical strains isolated from processed carcasses were also present at the breeder farm, providing molecular evidence for vertical transmission from breeder to broiler flocks. [^20]

*Salmonella* Pullorum provides a particularly well-characterized model of vertical transmission. Berchieri et al. (2001) showed that commercial laying hens inoculated with *S.* Pullorum at 4 days of age harbored the infection without morbidity until they came into lay, at which point they produced contaminated eggs and infected progeny. [^21] Genetic susceptibility played a role: inbred chicken lines with a SalI-susceptible phenotype showed greater localization of *S.* Pullorum in the reproductive tract than did SalI-resistant lines. [^21]

Haider et al. (2014) elucidated the mode of vertical transmission by experimentally infecting hens and cocks with *S.* Pullorum via the oral route. The bacteria invaded digestive epithelia, entered the bloodstream (bacteremia), and ultimately colonized the ovary and egg follicles, persisting there to contaminate laid eggs and subsequently hatched chicks. [^22] In cocks, *S.* Pullorum was detected in testicular tissue by PCR from one to three weeks post-infection. [^22]

Novel control strategies are being explored. Cui et al. (2023) demonstrated that oral administration of Salmonella phage CKT 1 to adult broiler breeders significantly reduced *S.* Pullorum colonization in the liver, spleen, heart, ovary, and oviduct, and importantly reduced bacterial loads on eggshells and in liquid whole eggs, thereby decreasing the probability of vertical transmission. [^23]

**Core Mechanisms of Pathogen–Host Interactions in Vertical Transmission**

Vertical transmission requires pathogens to overcome formidable host barriers. In mammals, the placental maternal–fetal interface employs multiple defense layers: the syncytiotrophoblast forms a continuous barrier lacking intercellular junctions, secretes antiviral cytokines including interferon-gamma, and expresses Toll-like receptors that link innate and adaptive immune responses. [^1] From the second trimester, IgG receptors on the syncytiotrophoblast actively transport protective maternal antibodies to the fetus. [^1] Pathogens that achieve vertical transmission have evolved specific strategies to breach or circumvent these barriers.

For CSFV, the critical factor appears to be replication speed: the virus achieves transplacental transmission before the maternal antibody response is mounted, exploiting a temporal window in the immune defense. [^13] For ALV-J, the virus exploits normal cellular machinery — semen extracellular vesicles and the tetraspanin CD 81 receptor — to gain entry to reproductive tissues and achieve transmission to progeny. [^7][^11]

In avian hosts, Salmonella exploits SPI-1-encoded T 3 SS effectors to invade intestinal epithelium, disseminate systemically via bacteremia, and colonize the reproductive tract — ovary and oviduct in hens, testes in cocks. [^17][^22] Host genetic background modulates susceptibility to reproductive tract colonization, as demonstrated by the differential *S.* Pullorum persistence in SalI-susceptible versus SalI-resistant chicken lines. [^21]

At the evolutionary level, immune genes represent hotspots of shared positive selection across birds and mammals, and genes upregulated in response to pathogens are enriched for signatures of positive selection, suggesting that pathogens consistently target the same host pathways across divergent clades. [^24] Immune evasion mechanisms are widespread and generate a spectrum of pathogenic effects, creating an evolutionary arms race that shapes both host defense and pathogen transmission strategies. [^25]

**Summary and Outlook**

Several key findings emerge from this survey. First, vertical transmission operates through diverse mechanisms across pathogen types: transovarial passage in fleas, transplacental infection in mammals, and reproductive tract colonization and egg contamination in birds. Second, the efficiency of vertical transmission is modulated by pathogen virulence (as seen with CSFV strain differences), host genetics (as shown in chicken SalI resistance studies), and specific molecular interactions (ALV-J use of extracellular vesicles and CD 81). Third, vertical transmission can serve ecological functions beyond simple host-to-offspring passage — as exemplified by the potential role of transovarial *Y. pestis* transmission in fleas as an inter-epizootic persistence mechanism.

Notable knowledge gaps remain. The molecular receptors and cellular pathways by which *Y. pestis* invades flea reproductive tissues and survives through metamorphosis are not yet characterized. For CSFV, the specific placental cell types targeted during transplacental infection and the viral determinants that govern tropism for fetal tissues require further investigation. In Salmonella vertical transmission, the relative contributions of trans-ovarian (before shell formation) versus trans-shell (after laying) routes to egg contamination under field conditions remain debated, and the interactions between host immune genotype and bacterial virulence gene repertoire in determining reproductive tract colonization efficiency are incompletely understood.

Future research directions include: (1) detailed molecular characterization of pathogen interactions with reproductive tissues across host species, using single-cell transcriptomics and spatial genomics approaches; (2) development of intervention strategies that specifically target vertical transmission routes, including phage therapy for Salmonella, nanobody-based approaches for ALV-J, and improved vaccines for CSFV; (3) ecological modeling incorporating transovarial transmission in arthropod vectors to better predict pathogen persistence and outbreak dynamics; and (4) comparative genomic studies to identify conserved host defense pathways that pathogens must overcome for successful vertical transmission across mammalian and avian systems.

**哺乳动物和鸟类中病原体的垂直传播：机制、主要病原体及宿主相互作用**

**引言**

垂直传播指的是病原体从父母传给后代的过程，包括几种不同的途径：经胎盘传播、经卵巢传播，以及通过生殖道传播——后者包括通过精液或在分娩期间传播。[^1]在哺乳动物中，胎盘构成了物理、分子和免疫层面的屏障，通常能保护正在发育的胎儿。然而，许多病原体已经演化出突破这些防御机制的手段。[^1]在鸟类中，垂直传播通常是通过感染卵子实现的——要么是在卵壳形成前病原体在卵巢和输卵管中定植，要么是在卵子产下后穿透卵壳。在跳蚤和蚊子等节肢动物媒介中，病原体从成虫经卵巢传播给卵子，从而使病原体能够在没有脊椎动物宿主的情况下在多个世代中持续存在。

**跳蚤的垂直传播机制**

跳蚤是鼠疫杆菌的常见传播媒介，而跳蚤通过卵进行传播的可能性长期以来一直存在争议。2024 年，Pauling 等人在《自然-通讯》上发表的一项开创性研究证明：实验室培育的、感染了鼠疫杆菌的东方鼠蚤，能够将活菌从成虫传递给卵子，而且这些细菌能存活于后续的所有发育阶段——幼虫、蛹以及新孵化的成虫。[^2]研究者认为，在哺乳动物宿主没有出现鼠疫症状的时期，跳蚤的卵传播机制有助于鼠疫杆菌在环境中的持续存在。[^2]这一发现解决了鼠疫生态学中的一个长期疑问：即当啮齿类宿主没有感染迹象时，该病原体是如何在两次疫情爆发之间存活下来的。

Hinnebusch 在 2005 年对跳蚤传播细菌的生物学机制进行了综述。他阐述了鼠疫耶尔森菌是如何通过水平基因转移获得两种特定于该菌的质粒，并利用内源染色体基因来适应跳蚤传播的。[^3] 跳蚤叮咬传播细菌的过程依赖于细菌在跳蚤前胃中形成生物膜。这种生物膜会物理性地阻断正常的吸血过程，迫使细菌在尝试吸血时反流进入哺乳动物宿主体内。[^4][^5] 关于这种依赖生物膜的传播机制的分子和遗传学基础已有了大量研究，其中包括 hmsHFRS 基因座、鼠疫耶尔森菌产生的毒素以及 Hfq 调控蛋白的作用。[^6][^5]

重要的是，Pauling 等人 2024 年关于经卵传播的研究结果表明，还存在一种独立于传统“跳蚤阻断模型”的维持机制——这一机制有助于解释病原体在静止期的存活问题，同时也能解释那些仅通过哺乳动物宿主动态难以解释的病原体储存现象。[^2]

**病毒的垂直传播**

*禽白血病病毒 (ALV)*

本综述考察了三种病原体的垂直传播——由跳蚤传播的节肢动物源性细菌、病毒和细菌（重点强调沙门氏菌）——重点关注哺乳动物和鸟类系统中病原体-宿主相互作用的机制。

禽白血病病毒 J 亚群（ALV-J）是一种高度致癌的逆转录病毒。自 20 世纪 90 年代末以来，该病毒已对全球家禽产业造成了严重破坏。垂直传播是该病毒代代相传的主要途径。[^7] 廖等人（2022 年）发现了一种新的传播机制：ALV-J 利用宿主精液中的细胞外囊泡作为垂直传播的载体。从受感染公鸡中分离出的精液细胞外囊泡含有 ALV-J 的基因组 RNA 和病毒蛋白。人工授精实验表明，这些细胞外囊泡能够将 ALV-J 传染给无特定病原体的母鸡，而这些母鸡又会将病毒垂直传染给后代雏鸡。[^7] 李等人（2017 年）进一步证实，通过人工授精水平感染了 ALV-J 的母鸡，能够通过鸡蛋将病毒传染给后代。[^8]

比较研究显示，ALV-J 的垂直传播似乎比水平传播更具危害性。Lin 等人（2013 年）发现，与水平感染的家禽相比，垂直感染父母所生的后代表现出更高的病毒排出率、淋巴细胞中的病毒整合率，同时胚胎的孵化率更低，后代的死亡率也更高。[^9]

近期的干预措施主要针对垂直传播途径。Liang 等人（2025 年）的研究表明，表达重组纳米抗体的转基因嵌合鸡能够降低 ALV-J 的垂直传播程度；在那些该抗体表达水平较高的母鸡中，甚至可以完全消除 ALV-J 的垂直传播。[^10] 同样，Liao 等人（2021 年）在动物模型中发现，抗 CD 81 抗体能够抑制 ALV-J 的感染并阻止其垂直传播，这说明四跨膜蛋白 CD 81 在 ALV-J 的侵入和传播过程中起着重要作用。[^11]

*经典猪瘟病毒（CSFV）*

经典猪瘟病毒是哺乳动物经胎盘传播病毒的研究中最典型的例子之一。Dewulf 等人（2001 年）对怀孕的母猪进行了实验性感染研究，结果表明：在妊娠 43 天至 67 天之间被感染的母猪中，病毒均会垂直传播给胎儿，从而导致流产和/或胎儿木乃伊化。[^12]

感染病毒的毒力强弱会显著影响经胎盘传播的效率与结果。Bohórquez 等人（2020 年）在妊娠中期用高毒力、中毒力或低毒力的 CSFV 病毒株感染母猪，发现高毒力和中毒力病毒株能够实现有效的经胎盘传播，而低毒力病毒株则导致的病毒 RNA 载量较低，经胎盘传播的比例也更低。[^13] 关键的是，经胎盘传播发生在母体抗体反应开始之前（母体抗体反应在感染后 14 天开始），这说明病毒的快速复制速度超过了免疫反应，在免疫系统尚未完全建立的脆弱时期实现了胎儿感染。[^13] 经胎盘感染后，胎儿的免疫系统确实能够识别 CSFV，这一点可从病毒血症胎儿中 CD 8+ T 细胞亚群数量的增加以及 IFN-α反应的增强得到证实。[^13]

已专门制定了疫苗接种策略来预防 CSFV 的垂直传播。Coronado 等人（2024 年）的研究表明，将重组 FlagT 4 G 疫苗单次接种给怀孕母猪后，能够引发强烈的中和抗体反应，并有效阻止了高毒力 CSFV 毒株的经胎盘传播。[^14]同样，通过植物表达方式制成的 E 2 糖蛋白亚单位疫苗在双次接种后也显示出预防垂直传播的效果。[^15]

胎盘传播 CSFV 的流行病学意义一直存在争议。Kaden 等人（2005 年）对一个地方性感染地区的怀孕野猪进行了研究，未发现通过胎盘传播而持续感染的幼年野猪在 CSFV 的持续存在中起关键作用。这表明，有其他因素对维持野猪群体中的病毒传播更为重要。[^16]

**细菌的垂直传播：以沙门氏菌为例**

沙门氏菌肠炎亚种仍然是全球食源性疾病中的主要致病菌。在禽类中，垂直传播是受污染的蛋类和肉类进入人类食物链的关键途径。[^17]无论是水平传播（通过环境）还是垂直传播（从亲代传给后代），都是鸡类感染沙门氏菌的主要途径。[^17]

家禽中沙门氏菌致病的分子基础主要与沙门氏菌致病岛 1 和 2（SPI-1 和 SPI-2）有关。这些致病岛编码 III 型分泌系统（T 3 SS），该系统能将各种效应蛋白输送到宿主细胞内，从而干扰宿主细胞的正常功能，并帮助细菌进入含有沙门氏菌的液泡中。[^17][^18]从肉鸡体内分离出的沙门氏菌菌株中发现了包括*lpfA*、*csgA*、*sitC*、*sipB*、*sopB*、*sopE*和*sivH*在内的毒力基因，而从人类身上分离出的沙门氏菌则携带更多种类的毒力因子。[^19]

家禽中沙门氏菌的垂直传播现象已在集约化生产系统和实验模型中得到研究。Liljebjelke 等人（2005 年）利用脉冲场凝胶电泳技术，追踪了鼠伤寒沙门氏菌和肠炎沙门氏菌在集约化肉鸡生产系统中的传播情况。研究结果表明，从加工后的鸡尸中分离出的相同菌株也存在于种鸡场中，从而为沙门氏菌从种鸡到肉鸡的垂直传播提供了分子生物学证据。[^20]

沙门氏菌普鲁拉姆亚种为研究垂直传播机制提供了极为理想的模型。Berchieri 等人（2001 年）的研究表明，4 日龄时被接种了沙门氏菌普鲁拉姆亚种的商业蛋鸡在发病前一直携带该病菌，但不会表现出任何症状。直到开始产蛋后，这些鸡才会产下受污染的鸡蛋，并将病菌传染给后代。[^21] 基因易感性在这一过程中也起着重要作用：具有 SalI 易感表型的近交系鸡，其生殖道中沙门氏菌普鲁拉姆亚种的分布比 SalI 抗性系更为集中。[^21]

Haider 等人（2014 年）通过让母鸡和公鸡经口腔途径感染沙门氏菌 Pullorum，阐明了该菌的垂直传播机制。该细菌侵入消化上皮细胞，进入血液系统（菌血症），最终在卵巢和卵泡中定植。它们会长期存在于这些部位，从而污染所产的鸡蛋，进而影响孵化的雏鸡。[^22]在公鸡中，感染后 1 至 3 周，可通过 PCR 技术在睾丸组织中检测到沙门氏菌 Pullorum。[^22]

人们正在探索各种新的控制策略。Cui 等人（2023 年）的研究表明，给成年肉鸡种鸡口服沙门氏菌噬菌体 CKT 1 后，能够显著降低肝脏、脾脏、心脏、卵巢和输卵管中的沙门氏菌污染程度。更重要的是，该措施还减少了蛋壳和整个鸡蛋中的细菌含量，从而降低了细菌的垂直传播概率。[^23]

**垂直传播中病原体与宿主相互作用的核心机制**

垂直传播需要病原体克服宿主设置的种种屏障。在哺乳动物中，胎盘这一母体与胎儿的连接处具有多重防御机制：合胞滋养层形成了没有细胞间连接的连续屏障，能分泌包括干扰素-γ在内的抗病毒细胞因子，同时还表达能连接先天免疫和适应性免疫反应的 Toll 样受体。[^1]从妊娠第二期开始，合胞滋养层上的 IgG 受体会主动将母体产生的保护性抗体输送给胎儿。[^1]那些能够实现垂直传播的病原体，都演化出了专门用来突破或规避这些屏障的策略。

对于 CSFV 而言，关键因素似乎是病毒的复制速度：该病毒能在母体产生抗体反应之前实现经胎盘传播，从而利用了免疫防御机制中的时间窗口。[^13] 对于 ALV-J 而言，该病毒利用了正常的细胞机制——精液中的细胞外囊泡以及四跨膜蛋白 CD 81 受体——来侵入生殖组织并实现向后代的传播。[^7][^11]

在禽类宿主体内，沙门氏菌利用 SPI-1 编码的 T 3 SS 效应蛋白侵入肠道上皮细胞，通过菌血症在体内扩散，并定植于生殖道——母鸡的卵巢和输卵管、公鸡的睾丸。[^17][^22]宿主的遗传背景会影响其对生殖道定植的易感性，SalI 敏感型与 SalI 抗性鸡品系中沙门氏菌的存活情况差异就证明了这一点。[^21]

在进化层面，免疫基因是鸟类和哺乳动物中共同经历正向选择的热点区域。那些在病原体刺激下表达增强的基因，也普遍具有正向选择的特征。这表明，不同演化支中的病原体总是针对宿主的相同机制发动攻击。[^24] 免疫逃逸机制十分普遍，会引发多种病理效应，从而形成一场进化军备竞赛，进而影响宿主的防御机制和病原体的传播策略。[^25]

**总结与展望**

这项调查得出了几项重要结论。首先，不同病原体的垂直传播机制各不相同：跳蚤通过卵传播，哺乳动物通过胎盘感染，鸟类则通过生殖道定植和卵子污染实现垂直传播。其次，垂直传播的效率受病原体毒力（如 CSFV 不同毒株之间的差异）、宿主遗传因素（如鸡对 SalI 的抗性研究所示）以及特定分子相互作用的影响（如 ALV-J 利用细胞外囊泡与 CD 81 的相互作用）。第三，垂直传播除了简单的宿主向后代的传播外，还具有其他生态功能——例如，跳蚤通过卵传播鼠疫耶尔森菌，可能有助于该病原体在动物群之间的持续存在。

仍存在一些重要的知识空白。关于鼠疫耶尔森菌如何侵入跳蚤的生殖组织并在变态过程中存活的分子受体和细胞通路，目前尚未明确。对于鸡脑炎病毒，其在经胎盘感染过程中所针对的特定胎盘细胞类型，以及决定其对胎儿组织亲和力的病毒因素，都需要进一步研究。在沙门氏菌的垂直传播方面，关于在野外条件下，通过卵壳形成前还是卵壳形成后的途径导致鸡蛋污染，目前仍有争议；同时，宿主免疫基因型与细菌毒力基因在决定细菌在生殖道中的定植效率方面的相互作用，也尚未完全明了。

未来的研究方向包括：(1)利用单细胞转录组学和空间基因组学技术，详细分析不同宿主物种中病原体与生殖组织的相互作用机制；(2)研发针对垂直传播途径的干预策略，包括用于沙门氏菌的噬菌体疗法、用于 ALV-J 的纳米抗体疗法，以及改进后的 CSFV 疫苗；(3)建立包含节肢动物媒介经卵传播机制的生态模型，以更准确地预测病原体的持续存在情况与疫情爆发趋势；(4)通过比较基因组学研究，识别病原体在哺乳动物和鸟类系统中实现垂直传播时必须克服的宿主防御机制。
